How this works (and its limits)

Android + Chrome: reads the real ambient light sensor (lux) when available. Otherwise the camera is used with manual (locked) exposure where supported — auto-exposure can't "correct away" the signal. Where lock is unavailable, it meters the camera's chosen ISO × shutter speed via the incident-meter equation E = C·N²/(t·ISO), with the aperture (N) read from a photo's EXIF. The constant C is platform-tuned (Android ≈ 1200, iOS ≈ 200): phone makers' auto-exposure targets differ ~5× in bright sun, so one textbook constant (250–330) under-reads Android and over-reads iPhone. When exposure lock succeeds, the pre-lock metered reading anchors the pixel scale per device, so the live and photo paths agree by construction.

iPhone / iPad: iOS exposes neither the light sensor nor exposure controls to websites, and its live camera preview is HDR tone-mapped (dark scenes are artificially lifted — absolute brightness is unreliable by design). Two paths: the live video gives a rough "% of clear sky" with a steeper correction curve; for a real measurement, use the 📸 Photo meter — it reads ISO, shutter speed and aperture from the photo's EXIF and applies the same incident-meter equation as Android, so iPhone and Android readings are directly comparable.

Why two phones read differently — and the fix: apertures (f/1.8–f/2.2 ≈ ±25% via the N² term), sensor gains and tone curves differ per device, and iPhones add HDR processing. SunD applies platform-specific corrections: on Android it locks exposure (or meters ISO×shutter with the EXIF aperture); on iOS it expands the compressed preview band for the live view and treats the 📸 Photo meter as the real measurement — the photo's EXIF (ISO, shutter, aperture) feeds the same incident-meter equation on every platform. Everything is converted to % of clear-sky brightness for the sun's exact current position, so readings are comparable across phones.

Live vs static: Android runs continuously off the live camera/sensor. On iPhone/iPad, snap the sky once — the app freezes that reading, turns the live camera off, and the session timer keeps accumulating from the photo's measured value until you retake it.

Phone sensors only see visible light, never UV. SunD estimates UV by comparing your measured brightness with the theoretical clear-sky brightness for the sun's exact position (computed from your time & location — no internet needed). Clouds and haze dim the light, and the estimate drops with it.

The golden rule: outdoors or at an open window, camera/sensor facing the sky. Glass blocks the UV-B that makes vitamin D but lets visible light through. Expect ±2–3 UV index of error: a guide, not a measurement.

The vitamin D calculation (with references)

Step 1 — UV dose rate. Erythemal (sunburn-weighted) irradiance = UVI × 25 mW/m², per the WHO/WMO UV index definition on the CIE erythema action spectrum (ISO/CIE 17166).

Step 2 — Time to 1 MED. Minimal erythemal dose ≈ 200 / 250 / 300 / 450 / 600 / 1000 J/m² for Fitzpatrick types I–VI (typical solar-simulator values; MED rises with skin type). So t(MED) = MED ÷ (0.025 × UVI) seconds.

Step 3 — Vitamin D. Holick's rule: exposing ¼ of the body to ¼ of 1 MED ≈ 1,000 IU of oral vitamin D (Holick; PubMed 20398766, “Holick's rule and vitamin D from sunlight”). Full-body 1 MED ≈ 10,000–25,000 IU (Holick 2011; Med J Aust 2011). SunD scales this by your skin-exposure slider — the same model used by the dminder app.

Honesty note: real synthesis varies ±50% or more with age, body fat, season, and your current vitamin D level. This is a published rule of thumb, not a lab measurement.
